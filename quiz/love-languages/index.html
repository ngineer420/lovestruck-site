What to actually do about it

A ranking is only worth having if it changes what you do on a Tuesday. Open the language that came out on top — for you or for your partner — and steal something from it this week. Six concrete things per language, all of them small enough to do without planning.

Words of affirmation

The failure mode here is vagueness. "You're amazing" is pleasant and forgettable; "the way you handled that call with your mum was impressive" is the one they'll still be thinking about at lunch. Specificity is the whole thing.

  • Name one specific thing before noon. Not a compliment about them as a person — a compliment about something they actually did. More along these lines.
  • Write an actual letter. If you hate writing, there's a structure that makes it a twenty-minute job rather than a blank-page ordeal — see how to write a love letter.
  • Keep decent paper in the house so leaving a note is a two-minute decision instead of a project. A Peter Pauper Press Letter Perfect stationery set is a step up from printer paper without tipping into fussy.
  • Use a pen you like. Sounds trivial; it's the difference between writing notes and meaning to. A LAMY Safari is the usual starting point.
  • Praise them to someone else while they can hear it. Overheard praise carries differently from praise delivered straight at them, and most people never get any.
  • Put the words on the gift. The first anniversary is literally paper, which makes it the easiest one to get right for this language — see the paper year.
Quality time

Being in the same room does not count and never has. What registers is undivided attention — which is why twenty genuinely focused minutes beats a whole evening spent side by side on two phones.

  • Put a recurring window in the calendar and defend it like you'd defend a meeting you can't move. Why protecting the time matters more than the plan.
  • Pick a date with nothing to look at but each other. The free ones are usually better at this than dinner, where the food does the work.
  • Play something cooperative so the evening isn't a competition. Codenames Duet is built for two players on the same side.
  • Keep a deck of questions for when it stalls. We're Not Really Strangers: Couples Edition gets past the logistics conversation fast.
  • Run a twenty-minute check-in at the same time each week. There's a format for it in the check-in questions guide.
  • Fix the attention, not the duration. Phones in another room, one thing at a time — more on that here.
Receiving gifts

This is the most misread of the five and the one people are most embarrassed to score high on. It isn't about money — it's about evidence. A gift proves you were paying attention weeks ago, which is exactly why an £8 thing they mentioned once beats an expensive thing they didn't.

  • Keep a running note on your phone of everything they mention wanting. This single habit does more than any amount of shopping. More small-gift ideas.
  • Give small and specific, more often, rather than saving up for one big correct gesture at Christmas.
  • Turn an ordinary night into an object. An Instax Mini 12 makes something they can stick on the fridge.
  • Print the year. A Mixbook photo book is the rare gift that gets better the longer you've been together.
  • Let the anniversary material give you a brief. A constraint beats a blank page — see gifting by relationship stage.
  • If it's three days out and you have nothing, there's a way to recover that isn't a gift card. Read this one.
Acts of service

The load-bearing word is unasked. Doing the dishes because you were asked to do the dishes reads as compliance; doing them because you noticed reads as love. Same dishes, completely different message.

  • Do the one task they've been dreading — the phone call, the form, the thing that's been on the list for a month. More of these.
  • Take a recurring chore off them permanently, not once. Once is a favour; permanently is a change to their week.
  • Remove the "what's for dinner" tax. A HelloFresh gift card lets them pick their own weeks rather than being signed up for something they didn't choose.
  • Plan the date end to end so they don't have to make a single decision. Start from the under-$20 list and just book it.
  • Show up during the bad week without being asked. What that looks like in practice.
  • Own the logistics of anything big. If you're moving, that means the van, the boxes and a BoxOps Label Hero labelling kit before they've thought about it.
Physical touch

Mostly not about sex. It's the hand on the back passing in the kitchen, sitting close instead of across, and the hug that runs a few seconds past automatic. It's also the first thing to quietly disappear during a stressful stretch, which is exactly when it's most missed.

  • Hold on a beat longer at the door, both leaving and arriving. It costs nothing and it's the one people notice stopping. More like this.
  • Touch in passing. A hand on the shoulder on the way to the kettle registers more than a scheduled cuddle.
  • Make the couch worth staying on. A YnM weighted blanket turns "watching something" into staying put under the same blanket.
  • Trade massages instead of booking a spa. A proper multi-piece set like the Farmers Body Spa Day at Home set makes it feel like an occasion rather than a stocking filler.
  • Build the date around being close. One blanket, one Picnic at Ascot backpack for two, somewhere with a view.
  • Watch for the drop-off under stress. When the week is hard, this is the first thing to go — the under-$50 dates are mostly built for sitting close.

How this quiz is scored

Five languages make ten possible head-to-head pairings. Each pairing comes up three times, which is where the 30 questions come from, and it means every language is offered exactly 12 times across the quiz. Your score for a language is simply how many of those 12 chances you took, and the five scores always total 30. Nothing is weighted and no answer is worth more than another — the balance is the entire design, because an unbalanced item set would hand the win to whichever language got asked about most.

The questions are situations rather than statements, and they're deliberately concrete: a bad week, a doorway, a Sunday morning with nothing on. Abstract self-assessment ("I value verbal appreciation") tends to measure what people believe about themselves. Asking what they'd actually pick on a specific evening measures something closer to the truth.

What the result is and isn't

It's a conversation starter with numbers attached. It is not a diagnosis, a personality type, or a fixed fact about you. As our longer piece on the framework goes into, the five-languages model came out of decades of counselling practice rather than controlled research, and attempts to test it since haven't found strong support for people sorting neatly into one primary category. Hold the result loosely.

What it's genuinely good for is the gap. If your partner scores 11 on a language you score 2 on, you have found — in about eight minutes, without an argument — the exact reason a kind thing you keep doing isn't landing. That's worth more than the label on top.

One honest caveat about the ranking: with 12 chances per language, a one- or two-point difference is noise. If two languages come out within two points of each other, treat them as tied rather than ordered.
